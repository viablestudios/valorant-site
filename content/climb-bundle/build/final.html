A NOTE FROM PEAKFORM

If you've bought a guide like this before and it didn't work, that's not necessarily on you. Most "improvement guides" are just aim tips wearing a cover page. Aim is maybe a third of why you're stuck. The rest is decisions, positioning, utility, and the fact that you probably haven't looked at your own gameplay with anything other than a scoreboard in months.

This book doesn't promise Immortal. Nobody selling you a PDF can promise that, and you should be suspicious of anyone who does. What it gives you is a way to find out what's actually wrong, fix two things at a time instead of everything at once, and check whether it's working — instead of queueing, losing, blaming your teammates or your crosshair, and doing it again tomorrow.

Iron to Immortal is a long way. Every rank on that road has a different problem. This book is built around that — not around one generic idea of "getting better."

WHY THIS WORKS

There's no secret here. Deliberate practice — identifying a specific weakness, drilling it, testing it under real conditions, then reviewing what happened — is the same structure behind skill improvement in most competitive domains, from chess to sport. It works because it replaces "playing a lot" with "playing with a target."

What this isn't: a medical or performance-enhancing claim, a promise about your reflexes, or a substitute for genuinely wanting to improve. Some of this is habit-building, and habits take longer than a weekend to stick. If you do the diagnostic in Chapter 2 and skip everything after it, this book won't do anything for you. The value is in actually running the loop, not in owning the PDF.

WHAT TO EXPECT

Week one feels great. New system, new focus, probably a couple of good games because you're paying attention again.

Week two is where most players quit. The novelty wears off, you have a bad session despite "doing everything right," and it's tempting to decide the system doesn't work. It isn't the system. It's the point in any practice curve where the obvious gains have already happened and the next ones are smaller and slower. Planned for. Keep going.

THE RULE FOR BAD DAYS

The Tick-Sheet Is a Record, Not a Courtroom Rule

Miss a day, miss a drill, have a session where none of it clicked — don't restart, don't try to "catch up" by doubling tomorrow, don't treat one bad day as evidence the whole thing is broken. Do today. If today's gone, do this Sunday. Then the next one, properly. A missed day is a blank box, not a verdict.

MAKE IT FIT YOUR LIFE

  • Play at odd hours / inconsistent schedule: the daily plan still works — "today" just means whichever session is your next one, not a calendar date.
  • Only get 2–3 games in on weekdays: use the shortest warm-up routine every time rather than skipping warm-up on short sessions — five focused minutes beats zero.
  • Play with a fixed squad, not solo queue: adapt the decision-making sections to team calls rather than individual reads — the underlying questions (what information do we have, what's our job this round) still apply.
  • Genuinely burnt out, not just tilted: if ranked stopped being fun weeks ago, that's not a drill problem. Take the break. This book will still be here when queueing feels like something you want to do again, not something you're doing out of obligation.

PART 1 — WHY YOU'RE ACTUALLY STUCK

Chapter 1: You Probably Don't Need Better Aim Yet

Here's the assumption almost every stuck player makes:

"I'm losing because my aim is bad."

Sometimes, sure. But check this list before deciding that's your problem, because for most players stuck below Ascendant, it isn't:

  • Taking fights with no advantage
  • Poor crosshair placement
  • Re-peeking the same angle after it's already gone wrong once
  • Moving while shooting
  • Not trading a teammate who just died in front of you
  • Bad positioning — holding angles you can't escape
  • Utility that ends the round still in your inventory
  • Playing aggressive the moment you get an advantage, instead of using it
  • Tilting after a mistake and making three more
  • Changing sensitivity every time a session goes badly

Aim is the easiest thing to blame because it's the easiest thing to see — you missed the shot, so that's obviously the problem. Harder to see: you were never in a winnable position to begin with, and no amount of crosshair discipline fixes a fight you shouldn't have taken.

Find out which one is actually you

Answer honestly — not how you'd like to play, how you actually played your last ten ranked games.

  1. When you die, is it usually because you missed a shot, or because you were caught somewhere with no way out?
  2. Do you know your deaths-per-map hotspot, or does every death feel like a surprise?
  3. After a bad round, do you carry it into the next one?
  4. Do you review your games, or move straight to the next queue?
  5. Can you name the two things you're actively trying to improve right now?
  6. Do you take a fight because it's a good fight, or because an enemy appeared?
  7. Have you changed your sensitivity in the last two weeks?
  8. When you get a kill, do you reposition, or hold the same spot expecting another?
  9. Do you play the same 1–2 agents, or pick based on mood?
  10. When a teammate makes a mistake, does it affect how you play the rest of the round?
  11. Could you describe your team's economy right now, mid-game, without checking?
  12. After a loss streak, do you keep queueing, or stop?

Tally your answers and find your type

The Aim Grinder — Good mechanics. Bad decisions. You can hit the shot; you just take fights you shouldn't and don't know why you're dying with a positive K/D some games and a terrible one the next.

The Overthinker — Knows the theory, hesitates in the moment. You could explain the correct play out loud, then freeze and do the opposite under pressure because thinking and doing aren't the same skill.

The Ranked Spammer — Plays eight games a day, reviews zero. Volume without feedback. You're not bad at learning — you've never given yourself anything to learn from.

The Coinflip Duelist — Either drops 30 or disappears completely. No middle setting. Your good games make you look better than your rank; your bad games make you look like you don't belong there. Both are you.

The Passive Player — Too scared to take space or make a mistake. You're not losing many duels because you're barely in any. Safe, and invisible, and stuck.

Most players are a mix of two. That's fine — it means you have two starting points instead of one. Write yours down; the rest of this book is aimed at whichever type you circled, not at a generic "better player."

PART 3 — MECHANICS THAT ACTUALLY MATTER

Chapter 3: Aim Without Living in Aim Trainers

Most players build mechanics backwards — chasing flicks first because flicks look good in clips, treating everything underneath as boring homework. Flip it:

Crosshair placement > movement > first-shot accuracy > micro-adjustment > flicking.

Not: crazy flicks > everything else.

If your crosshair is already at head height where the enemy is about to appear, you don't need a flick — you need a twitch of a few pixels. Flicking compensates for bad crosshair placement. Fix the placement and most "aim problems" shrink before you've trained a single flick.

[IMAGE: crosshair placement hierarchy diagram]

Covered in this chapter: mouse control, sensitivity and eDPI explained plainly, when to stop changing your sensitivity, counter-strafing, burst vs. tap vs. spray, micro-corrections vs. flicks, tracking moving targets, pre-aiming common angles.

Sensitivity — the short version: your eDPI (mouse DPI × in-game sensitivity) determines how far your hand moves for a given in-game turn. There's no universally correct number. There's a correct test: can you flick to a target roughly one screen-width away without overshooting more than once in ten tries? If yes, stop touching it. Constant sensitivity changes are usually tilt in disguise.

Three routines — pick one, not the biggest one

10-Minute Routine

  • 3 min: Range warm-up, first-shot accuracy on stationary bots
  • 3 min: Bot movement — track, don't flick
  • 4 min: Deathmatch, focused only on crosshair height, not kill count

20-Minute Routine

  • 5 min: Range warm-up
  • 5 min: Bot tracking + counter-strafing
  • 10 min: Deathmatch, alternating first-shot accuracy and repositioning after a kill

35-Minute Routine

  • 5 min: Range warm-up
  • 10 min: Bot tracking, flicking, micro-adjustment
  • 15 min: Deathmatch
  • 5 min: Review — write down one thing that felt off

The rule that matters more than any of the three above: the 35-minute routine isn't automatically "best." A player who does 10 minutes every day out-improves one who does 35 twice and quits. Pick the routine you'll still be doing in three weeks.

PART 4 — CROSSHAIR PLACEMENT

Chapter 4: Stop Making Your Mouse Do All the Work

Good players don't flick to every opponent. They make the opponent appear close to where they're already aiming.

Covered: head height, angle isolation, pre-aim, clearing corners, wide swings, off-angles, holding angles, distance from corners, predicting enemy movement.

[IMAGE: bad crosshair placement vs. good crosshair placement]

The 5-Game Crosshair Challenge

For five games, forget rank. Only concentrate on keeping your crosshair at head level. After every death, ask one question:

"How far did I have to move my mouse?"

If the honest answer is "a long way," that's not an aim problem — that's a placement problem, and it's the cheapest fix in this entire book.

PART 5 — WIN MORE GUNFIGHTS BEFORE THEY BEGIN

Chapter 5: Fight Selection

There's a difference between a good fight and a fight you technically can take.

Bad: get one kill, immediately swing another enemy. Better: get one, reposition, force them to clear you again.

Covered: numbers advantage, trading, double swinging, isolation, repositioning, using utility before fights, fighting with teammates, playing off contact, when not to peek.

THE 60/40 RULE

Try to create fights where you believe you have an advantage. You don't need every duel to be fair. You don't want fair fights — fair fights are a coinflip, and a coinflip isn't a plan.

PART 6 — DECISION MAKING

Chapter 6: Stop Autopiloting

Before the round, ask:

  • What is our economy? What is theirs?
  • What happened last round?
  • What utility do they normally use here?
  • What is my job this round?

Mid-round, ask what information you actually have — not what you think they're doing.

Covered: rotations, map pressure, numbers, timing, fake pressure, lurking, playing after a pick, when to speed up, when to slow down.

PART 7 — UTILITY

Chapter 7: Stop Dying With Everything Still Available

If the round ends and you're spectating with two flashes, a smoke, and your ultimate still in your inventory, congratulations — you've successfully saved them for next game.

Learn utility by purpose, not by memorising four hundred lineups:

  • Take space
  • Deny space
  • Create an advantage
  • Delay the enemy

Know why you're throwing something. Not "I saw this lineup on a clip somewhere."

Chapter 8: Where You Stand Changes Everything

Covered: cover, escape routes, off-angles, playing anti-flash, playing around teammates, avoiding exposed positions, retake positioning, post-plant positioning, defender positioning, attack spacing.

THE ESCAPE TEST

Before holding an angle, ask: "If three players swing me right now, where do I go?"

If the honest answer is "I guess I die" — your position isn't good, no matter how many kills it's gotten you before.

PART 9 — AGENT MASTERY

Chapter 9: Stop Changing Agent Every Loss

This isn't an agent encyclopedia. It's about building your agent pool:

  • 1 main agent
  • 1 secondary
  • 1 backup

Switching roles constantly makes improvement harder — you're relearning a different skillset every session instead of stacking reps on one.

Role Main responsibility Common low-rank mistake What to practise
Duelist Take space, create pressure Diving without info or support Entry timing, trading off your own picks
Initiator Create favourable fights Using utility on cooldown instead of on purpose Utility-into-fight sequencing
Controller Control pace and shape of the round Smoking the wrong choke, too early or too late Reading when to slow vs. speed the round
Sentinel Control territory, gather information Overextending off-site out of boredom Holding discipline, information calls

PART 10 — RANK-SPECIFIC ROADMAP

Different ranks have different problems. Giving a Bronze player Ascendant-level advice, or an Immortal player Iron-level advice, wastes both of their time.

IRON — Basic movement, stopping before shooting, crosshair height, understanding your agent, understanding maps. Don't worry about advanced tactics yet.

BRONZE — Crosshair placement, basic utility, avoiding random fights, playing with teammates, consistency.

SILVER — Trading, better movement, fight selection, economy awareness, basic communication.

GOLD — Positioning, utility timing, punishing enemy mistakes, map awareness, building consistent mechanics.

PLATINUM — Mid-round decisions, predictability, timing, better team play, repositioning.

DIAMOND — Small mistakes, tempo, utility combinations, reading opponents, advanced positioning.

ASCENDANT — Decision speed, consistency, adaptation, stronger communication, punishing patterns, discipline, reviewing tiny mistakes.

IMMORTAL — This is where raw fundamentals stop being the gap. Everyone here can aim, everyone here knows the callouts. What separates you now is decision speed under pressure — recognising a pattern in half a round instead of after three — and discipline: not taking the flashy line when the boring line wins. Practice here means fewer new concepts and more repetition of the same correct read until it's instant, not something you have to think through.

THE HIGHER YOU CLIMB, THE SMALLER THE MISTAKES BECOME. At Iron, fixing one fundamental can completely transform your games. At Immortal, climbing means fixing dozens of small decisions nobody else would even notice you got wrong.

PART 11 — HOW TO REVIEW YOUR OWN GAMES

Chapter 11: Become Your Own Coach

You don't need to watch every second of every VOD. Watch:

  • Every death
  • Every first engagement
  • Every lost advantage
  • Every clutch
  • Every round where you didn't know what to do

Ask five questions on each:

  1. Why was I here?
  2. What information did I have?
  3. Did I need to take this fight?
  4. Did I use my utility properly?
  5. What would I do differently next time?

PART 14 — BUILD YOUR IMPROVEMENT SYSTEM

The 5-Stage Loop

  1. Identify — what is holding you back?
  2. Practise — target that weakness.
  3. Play — apply it in ranked.
  4. Review — find what went wrong.
  5. Adjust — change the next practice session.

Then repeat. This replaces: queue > lose > queue > lose > change crosshair > queue.

THINGS HIGH-ELO PLAYERS DO THAT LOWER-ELO PLAYERS DON'T NOTICE

They reposition constantly. A lower-ranked player gets a kill and holds the exact same angle. A stronger player assumes "they know where I am now" and moves.

They rarely take completely random fights. The duel usually has context — utility, timing, a teammate, information.

They understand numbers. 5v4? You don't need a hero play. Sometimes doing less wins the round.

They clear angles deliberately. Not "spin around and hope nobody's there."

They adapt. If something fails three rounds in a row, they don't run it a fourth time because "surely it'll work this time."
